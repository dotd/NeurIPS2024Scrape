The UMI Arena. Handheld devices turn human demonstrations into robot policies. The arena is where those policies are put to the test, on the same tasks, the same robot, and the same rules.

Overview

Robot-free data collection, the founding idea of the Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI), has gone mainstream. A fast-growing open-source ecosystem (Fast-UMI, DexUMI, YUBI, and others) now produces manipulation data at scale. As UMI records end-effector-centric observations and actions, the resulting policies deploy across robots such as bimanual Franka, ALOHA, and humanoids.

The ecosystem has outgrown its measuring tools. There is no shared environment for evaluating the policies this data produces, and no common setting for comparing the handheld devices that collect it. This workshop builds one. Its centerpiece, the UMI Arena, pairs a large data release with a live competition on common hardware, so progress is measured on the same tasks under the same conditions. We treat the two sides of the ecosystem as equal pillars: policy evaluation through a competition, and device development through a hands-on exhibition.

Core Challenges

Progress in manipulation learning is hard to measure because there is no standard way to evaluate it. Each lab builds its own setup (robot, objects, tasks, metrics), so reported numbers rarely compare. A shared arena lets the community finally measure the questions the UMI ecosystem has opened. We organize the program around three of them:

  • Standardization. Can the community agree on one evaluation environment and protocol for real-world manipulation that is fair, meaningfully hard, and reproducible on common hardware?
  • Cross-embodiment deployment. Can one end-effector-centric policy run on arms it was never tuned for — Franka, TX-G2, OpenArm — over a task suite it has not seen?
  • Devices and data collection. Which handheld devices yield the largest, most diverse, highest-quality data, and how does device design shape the resulting policies?

The Three Tracks

The arena runs on one shared setup with three tracks, so policies, the devices that collect their data, and new community ideas meet on the same floor. Everything that can happen in advance does — policies are evaluated on our robots, exhibitor slots are selected, papers go through review — so the half-day itself is live robot demos, hands-on device trials, and short talks. Each track has a cash award funded by AIRoA. Track 1 · Policy Competition

Eight tasks and a public leaderboard let teams compare policies head-to-head on the same problems: five are published so teams can train and test against them, and three stay held out until the final evaluation. Teams submit trained models, and the organizers run all evaluation on shared robots, scoring every entry by task success rate. We expect 20–40 teams. The top three join a moderated Q&A on-site, and the winning team gives a live robot demo. The leaderboard stays open after the workshop.

→ Sign up to enter — registration closes Sep 30.

Competition dataset

We release a reference policy model with training code, so teams can fine-tune rather than train from scratch — links coming soon. We also release the low-cost, open-source YUBI data-collection hardware, with which a full real-robot setup can be reproduced for under $600. AIRoA releases the dataset in stages: an initial ~500 hours, already released on Hugging Face, and the full 15,000 hours by early September. The data spans manufacturing, retail, and home tasks. AIRoA collects it 24/7 across forty rigs running in parallel — a standing operation rather than a one-off campaign, which is what makes a release on this scale possible, and what will keep it growing after the competition. The dataset is shared with competition participants: sign up and we grant your team the full release.

Submission & evaluation

You train or fine-tune a policy — the reference model and its training code are released, so teams start from a working baseline rather than from scratch — and submit the trained model. Upload your trained policy to Hugging Face and send us the link — that is the whole submission.

Evaluation then runs in two stages. A simulator benchmark, released around September 1, scores every entry first, and the models that clear its success-rate threshold go forward to real robots (Franka, TX-G2, or OpenArm). Real-robot evaluation runs every two weeks through October, and teams can resubmit as often as they like — each round scores your latest model, so a weak early result costs nothing. Results appear on the public leaderboard.

Scoring covers eight tasks. Five are open, published ahead of time so teams can train and test against them. The remaining three are held out and appear only in the final evaluation, so the leaderboard measures generalization rather than practice on a known list.

The success-rate threshold is coming soon.

Track 2 · Device Exhibition

Groups set up their UMI-like data collection devices on-site. Attendees try them hands-on and vote on the spot via a single QR form on ergonomics, dexterity, strength, reproducibility, cost, etc. The winner receives Best Device, and the three most-voted devices give 5-minute lightning talks.

Applying takes two links:

  • A 2–4 page short paper that explains the device and its results.
  • A 1–3 minute video that explains the device and shows rollouts of policies trained on data your device collected.

Bring two things on the day:

  • At least one device, preferably the full rig required for data collection.
  • A laptop or monitor to present your device and play the video.

Exhibiting does not require the dataset agreement (NDA) — you show your own hardware and data. On-site we provide table space with power. A limited number of exhibition slots are selected by the organizers from the applications received, judged on the same criteria attendees vote on.

Applications close October 9, and we notify selected teams by October 16. At least one team member must attend in person on November 12 to staff the table, and to give the 5-minute lightning talk if your device finishes in the top three.

Track 3 · Contributed Papers

We invite 4-page, non-archival papers on the open problems of UMI-style manipulation. Accepted papers appear as posters with a few spotlight talks, running alongside the device exhibition, and a Best Paper award (cash) rewards clear, reproducible work.

Topics of interest

Topics of interest include (but are not limited to):

  • Handheld, robot-free data-collection devices — ergonomic, dexterous, low-cost hardware design
  • Robust in-the-wild tracking and localization beyond fragile SLAM
  • The egocentric viewpoint gap between handheld collection and robot deployment
  • End-effector-centric policies and cross-embodiment transfer (single-arm, bimanual, mobile, humanoid)
  • Vision-language-action (VLA) models and diffusion policies for real-robot manipulation
  • Force and tactile sensing with multi-finger dexterity
  • Data scaling, curation, and quality for large-scale demonstration datasets
  • Standardized benchmarking, evaluation protocols, and reproducibility on shared hardware
  • Failure detection, recovery, and robustness in real-world deployment
  • Sim-to-real transfer and data-efficient learning for manipulation

Submission

Papers are 4 pages excluding references, non-archival, and reviewed single-blind (at least two reviews each). The submission deadline is October 16. The OpenReview submission venue and the formatting template are coming soon.

The organizers have run this kind of competition before. They designed the ICRA 2026 challenge "From Data to Decisions: VLA Pipelines for Real Robots", which evaluated 36 teams' VLA models on shared real-robot hardware with a public leaderboard. The UMI Arena scales that up, from one Toyota HSR at ~10,000 hours to UMI handheld data at 15,000 hours, and adds standardized real-world evaluation and a device exhibition.
