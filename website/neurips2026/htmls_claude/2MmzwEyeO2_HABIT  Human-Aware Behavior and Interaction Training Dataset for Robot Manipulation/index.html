HABIT: Human-Aware Behavior and Interaction Training Dataset for Robot Manipulation

Jaehwi Song, Suchae Jeong, Byeongguk Jeon, Sungdong Kim, Kimin Lee (Config, KAIST); Hyungmok Son (Config)

https://habit-dataset.github.io

Abstract (summary)

Existing large-scale robot demonstration datasets are collected without humans present. As a result, the policies trained on them do not learn human-aware behavior. HABIT is a dataset of more than 10K episodes, covering over 160 hours and 60 tasks, in which a human shares the workspace with the robot. The tasks are organized into three roles:

  • Collaborator: the human and robot do the task jointly.
  • Coworker: they do separate tasks in a shared space.
  • Supervisor: the human directs the robot.

Training on HABIT produces three behaviors that robot-only data does not: synchronization, yielding, and gesture grounding. It also enables fast adaptation to new interaction tasks.

1 Introduction (summary)

Vision-language-action models and world action models generalize across scenes, tasks and embodiments, but their training data lacks humans. Behaviors such as handing over a tool or pausing for a reaching hand cannot be demonstrated without a person present. HABIT addresses this gap with 10,563 episodes (164 hours, 60 tasks) of bimanual manipulation, each with a co-present human.

The authors fine-tune π0.5 and GR00T N1.6 on six tasks and compare against matched robot-only data. HABIT yields:

  • higher success rates;
  • emergent yielding, gesture grounding and synchronization;
  • faster adaptation to new tasks.

2 HABIT Dataset

2.1 Task Design

Following the human-robot interaction literature, tasks fall into the three roles:

  • Collaborator: direct physical interaction with spatiotemporal coordination.
  • Coworker: a shared space with no contact, where collision avoidance is required.
  • Supervisor: the human gives cues such as gestures that the robot must interpret visually.

Plain instructions proved too ambiguous, so each task is specified by a task workflow: a directed graph of human (Hi) and robot (Ri) subtask nodes, with edges giving the execution order. The setting is one human and one robot.

2.2 Workspace Setup

The robot is two Franka Research 3 arms with Robotiq grippers, teleoperated with Meta Quest 3 controllers. A front table serves as the shared workspace, and a side table is for human-only activities. Five synchronized RGB cameras record each episode:

  • two wrist cameras and one robot-side center camera, which are used for training;
  • one head-mounted human camera and one exocentric camera, which are released with the data.

2.3 Data Collection Protocol

Reactive interaction: each operator acts only after observing their partner, so every action is triggered by a cue that is visible in the recordings.

Behavior elicitation:

  • Safety-first yielding: the robot retracts whenever a collision is imminent.
  • Varied human speed, so the policy must match the partner's tempo.
  • Randomized wait time before pointing, so the policy attends to the gesture rather than acting on language alone.

Diversification: clothing color, object order and operators are varied.

2.4 Dataset statistics

Episodes average 3.67 robot and 4.30 human subtasks. There are 157 distinct robot subtasks, 182 distinct human subtasks and 308 unique human-robot pairs. Collaborator and Supervisor workflows are dominated by cross-agent edges, while Coworker workflows are dominated by single-agent edges. The mean episode length is 59.9 s.

3 Evaluation Framework

Each robot subtask is scored on three binary criteria: manipulation, workflow compliance and human safety. A trial succeeds only if every robot subtask passes all three.

\[ \text{Success rate} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}[\tau_i \text{ succeeds}] \tag{1} \]

The six evaluation tasks are:

  • Collaborator: Table Serving and Shelf Cleaning.
  • Coworker: Waste Sorting (moderate overlap, about 2 yield events per trial) and Box Packaging (high overlap, about 3).
  • Supervisor: Donut Serving and Food Storage.

4 Experiments

4.1 Experimental Setup

π0.5 and GR00T N1.6 are each fine-tuned for 5,000 steps at batch size 128. The baseline is matched robot-only data collected without a human; it does not apply to Collaborator tasks. Each condition uses about 200 episodes, and each cell is evaluated with N = 20 trials.

4.2 Main Results

HABIT improves success on every comparable task. The largest gains are on Coworker tasks. Donut Serving is comparable across conditions because the language index alone suffices. Food Storage gains from the gesture cue at the two-stage branching point.

4.3 Failure Analysis

Each role has a characteristic failure mode: precondition violation for Collaborator, collision for Coworker, and gesture-following failure for Supervisor. HABIT substantially reduces all three.

4.4 Sample-Efficient Adaptation to New Tasks

π0.5 is mid-trained on HABIT with the evaluation tasks excluded, then fine-tuned on new tasks. This improves both sample efficiency and final performance:

  • On Waste Sorting, 50 demonstrations with mid-training match 200 demonstrations without it.
  • On Shelf Cleaning, 100 demonstrations with mid-training reach 60%, versus at most 45% for direct fine-tuning.

5 Related Work (summary)

Large robot datasets such as DROID, BridgeData V2, Open X-Embodiment and AgiBot World assume the robot is the only agent present. Prior HRI datasets either treat the robot as a teleoperated tool or use scripted robot behavior. HABIT instead provides policy-learning data in which the robot responds to an independent human.

6 Limitations (summary)

  • Data comes from a single environment with about ten operators.
  • Only one-to-one human-robot interaction is covered.
  • Real-world evaluation is hard to reproduce, although detailed evaluation protocols are published.

7 Conclusion and Future Directions (summary)

Human presence is a learnable and impactful axis of data diversity. Future work includes using the human-side camera views and testing whether the mid-trained prior transfers to robot-only fine-tuning.

Appendix (summary)

A Hardware and Collection Details

The two arm bases are at y = ±0.41 m. Teleoperation is based on DROID. Cameras record at 640×480 and 10 Hz. Each protocol component was motivated by failures observed during development; for example, with randomized wait times, policies waited for gestures delayed by up to 4 minutes.

B Object Diversity

The dataset uses 101 object types across 12 categories. Of these, 59 types appear in two or more tasks.

C Evaluation Task Details

The appendix specifies per-task setups, randomization, evaluation criteria, protocols and workflows. Supervisor wait-time bins are:

  • Food Storage: up to 30 s.
  • Donut Serving: up to 20 s.

D Model Training Details

Training runs on 8× H100 GPUs.

  • π0.5: learning rate 5×10−5 with cosine decay to 5×10−6, action horizon 10, EMA 0.999.
  • GR00T N1.6: top-4 LLM layers, projector and action head tuned; learning rate 1×10−4; horizon 16; color jitter augmentation.

G Mid-Training Experiment Details

Mid-training uses 41 tasks with up to 100 demonstrations each, batch size 256, and 16,500 steps. Fine-tuning steps are scaled with the number of demonstrations.

H Combining HABIT with Human-Absent Data

Co-training on Robot-only and HABIT data gives the best results both with and without a human present.

I Ethics Statement (summary)

  • Participants were staff who gave written consent and could withdraw.
  • An internal ethics review covered six risk categories.
  • Faces are blurred in the release, and data is stored with encryption and access controls.
  • Takedown requests are honored within 14 business days.
