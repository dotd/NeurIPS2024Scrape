SyncWorld: Visual Calibration Enables World Models as Zero-Shot Simulators

Yuncong Yang1*, Zhengtao Han2*, Furkan Ozyurt3*, Zeyuan Yang1, Han Yang1, Junyi Cao1, Haoyu Zhen1, Yilun Du4, Chuang Gan1

1UMass Amherst, 2UC Berkeley, 3NYU, 4Harvard — yuncongyang@umass.edu — https://umass-embodied-agi.github.io/SyncWorld/

* Equal Contribution. Preprint.

Abstract

[Summary] Action-conditioned world models used as policy-in-the-loop simulators need fine-grained action controllability. However, the same numerical action looks different in pixel space across environments, cameras, robot placements, and embodiments. This causes conflicting supervision during training and brittle deployment. SyncWorld addresses this by conditioning on a visual calibration episode, which specifies the setup's Action–Visual Mapping in context. Training with calibration also teaches the model to use interaction history when calibration is absent. As a result, SyncWorld simulates unseen setups zero-shot and enables training-free test-time policy improvement.

1 Introduction

[Summary] World models are increasingly used as "imagination environments" for rollouts and policy evaluation, which requires frame-level action control. Scaling them across heterogeneous robot data is hard because actions are not a universal pixel-space language. The Action–Visual Mapping depends on camera extrinsics, base placement, and embodiment. This causes (1) conflicting supervision and (2) non-adaptive test-time mapping. SyncWorld treats the mapping as a latent relation calibrated in context from a short episode covering all six motion DoFs. It is trained so that interaction history alone can approximate the mapping.

Contributions:

  • SyncWorld and visual calibration for scalable controllable world modeling.
  • History-based in-context adaptation via calibration distillation.
  • Zero-shot policy improvement through test-time scaling.

2 Related Work

2.1 Controllable Action-Conditioned World Models

[Summary] Video models are used to synthesize trajectories, infer actions, or jointly predict actions and video. Controllable world models support planning and evaluation, and some add explicit visual action representations such as ray maps, prompts, or masks. SyncWorld instead handles setup-dependent action semantics via in-context calibration.

2.2 Latent Action World Modeling

[Summary] Latent-action methods learn from unlabeled video, but their proxy actions need grounding and are not consistent under camera or controller shifts. SyncWorld models true low-level actions directly.

2.3 In-Context Adaptation of World Models

[Summary] Prior adaptation relies on explicit calibration or geometry, privileged supervision, test-time finetuning, or closed-loop refinement. SyncWorld adapts per setup purely in context.

3 Method

3.1 Calibration-based World Modeling

The interaction history is defined as:

\[H_t \triangleq \{(I_{t-L+1}, a_{t-L+2}), \dots, (I_{t-1}, a_t), I_t\} \tag{1}\]

A standard action-conditioned model predicts future frames from the history and an action chunk:

\[I_{t+1:t+H} \sim W_\theta(\cdot \mid H_t, A_t) \tag{2}\]

SyncWorld additionally conditions on a setup-specific calibration context:

\[I_{t+1:t+H} \sim W_\theta(\cdot \mid \mathcal{C}^s, H_t, A_t) \tag{3}\]

[Summary] For each setup, a calibration episode \(\mathcal{E}^s\) executes one random-sign motion per DoF and then returns to a nominal pose. From this episode, 12 signed segments are extracted.

3.2 Learning World Model with Visual Calibration

The calibration segments are arranged in a canonical order:

\[\mathcal{C}^s = [\mathcal{C}^s_{+x}, \mathcal{C}^s_{-x}, \mathcal{C}^s_{+y}, \mathcal{C}^s_{-y}, \dots, \mathcal{C}^s_{+\text{roll}}, \mathcal{C}^s_{-\text{roll}}] \tag{4}\]

[Summary] Two training designs support this:

  • Action-coordinate augmentations. Sign flips, axis permutations, and translation scaling are applied consistently to the calibration, history, and future actions. This forces the model to read action semantics from calibration.
  • Calibration distillation. A calibration-free student is trained to match the teacher that has calibration.

3.3 Zero-shot Policy Improvement via World-Model Ranking

Following GPC-Rank, \(K\) candidate action chunks are sampled from the policy:

\[A_t^{(k)} \sim \pi(\cdot \mid O_t, l),\quad k=1,\dots,K \tag{5}\]

Each candidate is rolled out in the world model:

\[\hat I^{(k)}_{t+1:t+H} \sim W_\theta(\cdot \mid \mathcal{C}^s, H_t, A_t^{(k)}) \tag{6}\]

Each rollout receives a score \(s^{(k)}=\text{VLMScore}(\hat I^{(k)}_{t+1:t+H}, l)\), and the selected candidate is \(k^\star=\arg\max_k s^{(k)}\).

3.4 Training Data

[Summary] Training data comes mainly from RLBench, RoboCasa, and RoboMimic, replayed under randomized cameras with calibration episodes. It also includes perturbed non-success rollouts and real-world DROID data, which has no calibration episodes.

4 Experiments

4.1 Experiment Setups

[Summary] Evaluation uses unseen ManiSkill and LIBERO (both Franka) and real xArm rollouts. There are 50/50/25 trajectories, each recorded from two views, giving 100/100/50 videos. The model generates at 512×512 with 16-step action chunks. It is trained on 32 H100s with batch size 64 for 2–3 days.

5 Conclusion

[Summary] Visual calibration in context lets one world model scale across heterogeneous data. It yields strong video quality, 3D consistency, and training-free policy improvement in unseen setups.

B Training Details

B.1 Setting Details

B.1.1 Model and Training Configuration

[Summary] The model is built on Wan2.2-TI2V-5B with its 4×16×16 VAE. Pose encoders and identity-initialized projectors are added per DiT block, plus a zero-initialized calibration metadata embedding. Each sample has 60 calibration frames (12 segments of 5 frames), 25 history frames, and 16 future frames at 512×512. Robot conditioning is 8-dimensional per frame, packed four frames per 32-dimensional token. Training uses bf16, a learning rate of 5×10−5, and per-device batch size 2.

B.1.2 Augmentation

With probability \(p_{\text{axis}}\), the same transform is applied to all pose streams: \(R'_t = R_t S,\ p'_t = s\,p_t\). Here \(S\) is a right-handed signed permutation matrix (\(\det S=+1\)) and \(s\sim\mathcal{U}(0.7,1.5)\). Identical photometric jitter is applied across all video streams.

B.1.3 Distillation

[Summary] The student input uses a null calibration: a black video, zero/identity poses, and flag −2.0. The distillation loss is:

\[\mathcal{L}_{\text{distill}} = \mathbb{E}_\tau\big[\|\hat\epsilon_\theta(\text{history},\text{null-calib}) - \text{sg}(\hat\epsilon_\theta(\text{history},\text{calib}))\|_2^2\big]\]

The total loss is \(\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{base}}+\lambda_{\text{distill}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{distill}}\).

B.1.4 Curriculum

[Summary] Training has two phases:

  • Phase 1: every sample is calibrated, with \(p_{\text{axis}}=0.3\).
  • Phase 2: mixed data including samples without calibration, with \(p_{\text{axis}}=0.6\) and distillation most active.

B.2 Dataset Generation

[Summary] The pipeline samples camera setups, records a calibration episode, replays the expert, optionally adds counterfactuals, and stores the results hierarchically.

B.2.1 Random Camera View Details

[Summary] Cameras are perturbed around the default pose, with optional orbital arcs. A view is accepted only if the gripper is visible, with up to 150 attempts. Backgrounds are also varied.

B.2.2 Calibration Episode Details

[Summary] Translation and rotation DoFs are shuffled independently, giving the order \((\pi_{\text{trans}}(1),\pi_{\text{trans}}(2),\pi_{\text{trans}}(3),\pi_{\text{rot}}(1),\pi_{\text{rot}}(2),\pi_{\text{rot}}(3))\). Each DoF is executed with a random sign in a forward–pause–reverse pattern: \(n_d\) steps of \(v^{(d)}\), then \(n_p\) zeros, then \(n_d\) steps of \(-v^{(d)}\).

B.2.3 Calibration segment extraction

[Summary] Steps are kept if they match the sign and exceed 1 mm or 0.2°. The contiguous run with the largest cumulative motion is selected, and N=5 frames are taken from its onset and re-anchored. The segments are then reordered canonically.

B.2.4 Generating Counterfactual Episodes

[Summary] Two modes are used. Scripted divergence keeps the expert actions for \(\tau\le p\) and replaces the remainder with one or two synthetic constant-motion segments. Noisy replay uses \(a^{cf}_{t+\tau}=a^\star_{t+\tau}+\varepsilon_{t+\tau}\) with sparse control points (4%, interval 24) and \(\sigma=0.2\).

B.2.5 Dataset Statistics

[Summary] The dataset totals about 166 h (~29,880 videos, ~8,300 unique trajectories, ~3.6 views each). About 54 h of this is real-world data.

C Evaluation Details

C.1 Inference Configuration

[Summary] Inference uses a stride of 16 frames, 512×512 resolution, and FlowMatchScheduler (shift 5, 20 steps). Tiled VAE and bf16 are used. Action classifier-free guidance is applied as:

\[\hat\epsilon = \hat\epsilon_\varnothing + \lambda_{\text{action}}(\hat\epsilon_{\text{cond}} - \hat\epsilon_\varnothing) \tag{7}\]

with \(\lambda_{\text{action}}=5.0\).

[Summary] With a close, steep camera, the coarse rotation trend is captured, but the gripper trajectory is unstable.

[Summary] The end-effector is accurate, but the unseen peg is blurry and misplaced.
