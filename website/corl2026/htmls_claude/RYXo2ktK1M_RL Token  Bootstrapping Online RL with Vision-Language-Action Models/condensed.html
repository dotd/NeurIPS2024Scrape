RL Token: Bootstrapping Online RL with Vision-Language-Action Models

Charles Xu1, 2, † Jost Tobias Springenberg1 Michael Equi1 Ali Amin1 Adnan Esmail1 Sergey Levine1, 2 Liyiming Ke1

https://www.pi.website/research/rlt

Abstract: Vision-language-action (VLA) models perform diverse manipulation skills out of the box, but real-world precision and speed require further RL fine-tuning. We introduce a lightweight method for sample-efficient online RL fine-tuning of pretrained VLAs with a few hours of real-world practice. We (1) adapt the VLA to expose an "RL token," a compact readout that preserves task-relevant pretrained knowledge and serves as an interface for online RL, and (2) train a small actor-critic head on this token to refine actions while anchoring the policy to the VLA. Across four real-robot tasks demanding millimeter-level precision, RLT speeds up the hardest part of the task by up to 3×, raises success rates significantly within minutes to hours, and can surpass human teleoperation speed on some tasks.

1 Introduction

General-purpose VLAs often struggle in the last millimeter of execution: motions are slow, pauses and retries are needed, and small errors at critical stages compound into failure. RL fine-tuning can improve exactly these error-sensitive stages that demonstrations cover poorly, but real-world robotics has tight budgets: episodes cost time and wear, and adaptation must fit within a few hours.

Conventional RL for foundation models [1, 2, 3] needs large-scale data, while data-efficient real-world RL [4, 5] trains small models that lose the VLA's generalization. The question is how to retain the VLA's generalization while achieving the efficiency of lightweight online RL.

We train the VLA to expose an RL token, a compressed representation making pretrained knowledge accessible to a lightweight online RL policy. The frozen VLA provides perception and action recommendations; a small actor and critic adapt the policy on the hardest task parts, using sample-efficient online RL with a regularizer anchoring the actor to the VLA so RL refines promising behaviors. RLT operates over chunked actions aligned with the VLA's action interface, shortening the effective horizon for temporal-difference learning under sparse rewards at high control frequencies.

On four tasks requiring millimeter or sub-millimeter precision (screw installation, zip tie fastening, Ethernet, and charger insertion), RLT improves success and speed within a few hours. Critical phases are sped up by up to 3×, and success rises, e.g., from 20% to 65% on screw insertion. On one of the most dexterous parts, RLT surpasses expert teleoperation speed while remaining reliable.

2 Related Work

Vision-language-action models. Behavioral cloning from large datasets dominates generalist manipulation policy training [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11], using action chunking [12] and expressive action distributions [13] or autoregressive generation [6]. Pretrained vision-language backbones yield VLAs [6, 7], and combining them with chunked, expressive action generation achieves state-of-the-art results [8, 14, 15]. Despite strong generalization [16, 9], per-task performance is bounded by teleoperation data quality.

Real-world reinforcement learning (RL). RL can exceed demonstrations by discovering faster, more precise strategies. Off-policy actor-critic methods [17, 18, 19, 20] reuse replay data, with higher update-to-data ratios [21] improving efficiency, sometimes needing regularization [22], and can incorporate demonstrations [23]. Practical recipes include autonomous data collection [24], SERL [25, 4], RL100 [5], and human-in-the-loop corrections [4], solving contact-rich tasks within hours. However, they train small policies from scratch, forgoing VLA priors.

RL fine-tuning of VLA models. Full-model methods include RECAP [3], which trains \(\pi^*_{0.6}\) end-to-end with offline RL via advantage-conditioned policy extraction, and PPO-based fine-tuning [26, 27, 1]; both scale poorly to sample-efficient real-world RL. Lightweight methods freeze the VLA: ConRFT [28] fine-tunes an action head with a consistency objective on single-step actions; Policy Decorator [29] adds a hand-scaled residual, shown only in simulation with millions of steps; PLD [30] pre-trains a Cal-QL [31] critic then learns a single-step residual; GR-RL [2] and DSRL [32] steer diffusion noise toward high-return regions.

3 Preliminaries

Vision-language-action models. We build on the \(\pi_{0.6}\) model [33]. Given up to four camera images, a language instruction \(\ell\), and proprioceptive state \(\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{p}}_t\), \(\pi_{0.6}\) produces an action chunk: \(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{t:t+H-1} = (\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_t, \ldots, \tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{t+H-1}) \in \mathbb{R}^{H\times d}\), a sequence of \(H = 50\) actions covering 1 s of control. We write \(\pi_{\text{vla}}\) for the pretrained VLA's chunked policy. The robot executes a prefix (e.g., 20 steps) open loop before re-planning.

Reinforcement learning and actor-critic methods. We use an MDP \((\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, p, r, \gamma)\), where \(\mathcal{S}\) is the state space, \(\mathcal{A}\) a continuous action space, \(p(\mathbf{s}_{t+1} \mid \mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)\) the dynamics, \(r(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)\) the reward, and \(\gamma \in [0, 1)\) the discount. RL learns a policy \(\pi(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t)\) maximizing \(J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\rho_\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t r_t\right]\), where \(\rho_\pi(\tau)\) is the trajectory distribution induced by \(\pi\). The action-value function of \(\pi\) is \(Q^\pi(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\rho_\pi}\left[\sum_{t'=t}^{T} \gamma^{t'-t} r_{t'} \,\middle|\, \mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t\right]\).

Policies and critics operate over action chunks \(\mathbf{a}_{t:t+C-1} = (\mathbf{a}_t, \ldots, \mathbf{a}_{t+C-1}) \in \mathbb{R}^{C\times d}\), where \(C\) is the RL chunk length (and \(H\) the VLA chunk horizon). We choose \(C < H\) for reactivity. The chunked policy is \(\pi(\mathbf{a}_{t:t+C-1} \mid \mathbf{s}_t)\) with chunk-level C-step value \(Q^\pi(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_{t:t+C-1}) = \sum_{t'=t}^{t+C-1} \gamma^{t'-t} r_{t'} + \gamma^C \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}'\sim\pi|\mathbf{s}_{t+C}}\left[Q^\pi(\mathbf{s}_{t+C}, \mathbf{a}')\right]\). We build on off-policy actor-critic methods [34, 17, 19] training a stochastic actor \(\pi_\theta\) and critic \(Q_\psi\) from a replay buffer \(\mathcal{B}\), essential since \(\mathcal{B}\) aggregates data from the VLA, the RL learner, and human interventions.

4 Reinforcement Learning from RL Token

Figure 1 summarizes RLT. Training the whole VLA with online RL is too compute- and sample-inefficient for a few hours. Instead, the frozen VLA provides the state representation, reference actions, and exploration guidance, while a small actor and critic learn. We first adapt the VLA on a small task-specific demonstration set to improve its policy and expose an RL token; then we freeze it and train lightweight off-policy actor and critic networks conditioned on the RL token and VLA reference actions, regularizing the policy toward the VLA. Online RL thus becomes local refinement rather than unconstrained search.

4.1 Adapting the VLA to expose an RL interface

Online RL efficiency depends on state representation. The full VLA representation is high-dimensional and updating billions of parameters online is expensive and sample-inefficient, yet the pretrained representation contains useful knowledge. We therefore compress it into a compact embedding for RL.

We add an RL token (Fig. 2): a learned readout embedding summarizing the VLA's knowledge into a small vector. We train a small encoder-decoder transformer [35] on top of the VLA, with the RL token as the last encoder input; since it must let the decoder reconstruct the inputs, it acts as a bottleneck.

Let \(\mathbf{z}_{1:M} = \{\mathbf{z}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{z}_M\}\) be the final-layer VLA token embeddings for state \(s\), with each \(\mathbf{z}_i\) the embedding of one input token. We append a learned embedding \(\mathbf{e}_{\text{rl}} = \mathbf{e}_\phi(\texttt{<rl>})\) and process the sequence with a lightweight encoder \(g_\phi\). The encoder output at the special-token position is the RL token \(\mathbf{z}_{\text{rl}} = g_\phi\big([\mathbf{z}_{1:M}, \mathbf{e}_{\text{rl}}]\big)_{M+1}\). A decoder \(d_\phi\) with linear projection \(h_\phi\) autoregressively reconstructs the embeddings from \(\mathbf{z}_{\text{rl}}\). With stop-gradient \(\bar{\mathbf{z}}_i = \mathrm{sg}(\mathbf{z}_i)\) on VLA embeddings, the reconstruction objective on demonstrations \(\mathcal{D}\) is:

\[\mathcal{L}_{\text{ro}} = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{M} \big\|h_\phi\big(d_\phi([\mathbf{z}_{\text{rl}}, \bar{\mathbf{z}}_{1:i-1}])\big)_i - \bar{\mathbf{z}}_i\big\|^2\Big]. \tag{1}\]

We train \(\phi\) on a small task-specific dataset with the VLA frozen with respect to \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{ro}}\), optionally combined with supervised VLA fine-tuning (\(\theta_{\text{vla}}\)). Then \(\theta_{\text{vla}}\) and \(\phi\) are frozen, and online RL uses \(\mathbf{z}_{\text{rl}}\).

4.2 Online RL to refine VLA action chunks

We train lightweight actor (\(\pi_\theta\)) and critic (\(Q_\psi\)) networks online. Inputs \(x\) combine the RL token with information like proprioceptive state. The critic estimates \(Q_\psi(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{a}_{1:C}) \in \mathbb{R}\). The actor \(\pi_\theta(\cdot|\mathbf{x}, \tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{1:C})\) refines VLA-proposed action chunks \(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{1:C}\) rather than generating from scratch.

Training the critic. The critic \(Q_\psi(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{a}_{1:C})\) takes the state and action chunk \(\mathbf{a}_{1:C}\) and is trained with off-policy TD learning on chunk transitions from \(\mathcal{B}\):

\[\mathcal{L}_Q = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}_{1:C},\mathbf{x}')\sim\mathcal{B},\mathbf{a}'\sim\pi_\theta(\mathbf{x}')}\Big[\Big(\sum_{t'=1}^{C} \gamma^{t'-1} r_{t'} + \gamma^C\Big[Q_{\psi'}(\mathbf{x}', \mathbf{a}')\Big] - Q_\psi(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{a}_{1:C})\Big)^2\Big] \tag{2}\]

where \(\mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{z}_{\text{rl}}, \mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\), \(\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{p}}\) is proprioceptive state, \(\mathbf{z}_{\text{rl}}(\mathbf{s})\) the RL token for state s, \(\mathbf{x}'\) the next state, and \(\mathbf{a}' \sim \pi_\theta\) a sample from the RL policy. Following TD3 [19], \(\psi'\) are target network parameters.

Training the RL Policy. The actor \(\pi_\theta(\cdot|\mathbf{x}, \tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{1:C})\) takes state \(\mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{z}_{\text{rl}}, \mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\) and a VLA reference chunk \(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{1:C}\) and outputs a Gaussian over action chunks. Conditioning on \(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}\) lets RL refine a strong proposal and preserves mode information from the VLA's multimodal distribution that a unimodal Gaussian struggles to recover [36]. We maximize critic value while staying close to the reference chunk \(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}\), akin to KL-regularized RL [37, 38, 20, 39, 40], turning RL into local action editing. The objective is

\[\mathcal{L}_\pi(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathbf{s}\sim\mathcal{B}\\ \mathbf{a}_{1:C}\sim\pi_\theta}}\Big[-Q_\psi(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{a}_{1:C}) + \beta\,\|\mathbf{a}_{1:C} - \tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{1:C}\|_2^2\Big] \tag{3}\]

where \(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{1:C} \sim \pi_{\text{vla}}(\cdot \mid \mathbf{s}, \ell)\) and \(\beta\) controls regularization strength. Since the actor tends to copy \(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}\), we apply reference action dropout, zeroing a random subset of the reference chunk during training, forcing an independent action pathway while still exploiting the VLA when available.

4.3 The Complete System

We pre-fill the replay buffer with base VLA rollouts, then collect autonomous experience while performing asynchronous updates, with 10 critic and 5 actor updates per data point. A human provides sparse success labels and may intervene with teleoperated commands \(\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{h}}_{1:C}\) overriding the actor; interventions replace the VLA reference in the buffer.

Targeted improvement of critical phases. RLT is applied only to each task's critical, high-precision phase; the base VLA handles easier parts. A human operator chooses when to hand control to the RL policy, analogous to interactive imitation learning [41], concentrating data and credit assignment where adaptation matters. For autonomous test-time execution, a short final VLA fine-tuning predicts the handover point using interventions as labels. Algorithm 1 summarizes training.

5 Real-World Experiments

We test whether RLT improves the critical contact-rich phases under limited interaction time, sparse supervision, and lightweight learning. Q1: Does RLT improve over the base VLA? Q2: How does it compare to alternative RL approaches? Q3: How much does each component contribute? Q4: Does RLT discover better strategies than the demonstrations?

5.1 Tasks and Setup

We use four tasks (Fig. 3) on a bimanual robot at 50 Hz. (1) Screw installation: driving an M3 screw with an electric screwdriver; hardest, requiring sub-millimeter alignment with arbitrary grasp poses, where small rotations are amplified over the ~10 cm to the tool tip. (2) Zip tie fastening: threading a zip tie tail through its slot, a bimanual deformable task with millimeter tolerances. (3) Ethernet insertion: inserting a connector into a recessed port with accurate alignment and decisive motion. (4) Charger insertion: inserting a charger into a power strip, with prongs and socket often occluded.

Critical-phase evaluation. Each task's critical phase—insertion or fastening—is where precision is highest and the base VLA most often slows or fails, lasting 5–20 s (250–1000 steps). Episodes reset to a state right before this phase.

Full-task evaluation. The robot starts at home, the base policy executes earlier stages, and RL must handle the broader state distribution this induces—a substantially harder setting.

Data and Training Starting from pretrained \(\pi_{0.6}\) [33], we collect 1–10 hours of demonstrations per task, fine-tune the VLA and train the RL token, then run 400–1000 RL episodes. We report success rate and throughput (successes per 10 minutes) over 50 trials, on critical phases for all tasks and full tasks for screw and zip tie. Details in App. B.2.

5.2 Baselines and Ablations

Baselines: HIL-SERL [4] trains a small actor-critic with experience and interventions, without a VLA prior. Probe-Learn-Distill [30] learns a scaled single-step residual added to VLA actions. DSRL [32] learns RL in the flow VLA's latent noise space. DAgger [42, 41] fine-tunes the VLA on our collected intervention data. Details in App. B.3.

Ablations: w/o RL token: frozen ImageNet ResNet-10 encoder from [25]. w/o Chunk: single-step actions (\(C\)=1); since querying the VLA at 50 Hz is infeasible, the ResNet-10 encoder replaces the RL token. w/o BC Regularizer: \(\beta\)=0 in Eq. (3), training with the \(Q\)-function alone. w/o Pass-Through: remove \(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}\) from actor input.

5.3 Experimental Results

Q1: Does online RL improve over the base VLA policy?

Online RL improves success rate and speed in both settings (Fig. 4, Fig. 5 in Appendix). On charger and Ethernet, RLT is about 3× faster in the critical phase; success gains are larger on zip tie and screw. In full tasks, success is lower due to earlier compounding errors, but RLT still improves success by 40% on screw and 60% on zip tie.

Q2: How does RLT compare to alternative methods?

RLT significantly boosts throughput over baselines (Fig. 6) on Ethernet and a novel charger configuration. Single-step methods HIL-SERL and PLD fail at 50 Hz over hundreds of steps, as sparse reward does not propagate without chunking. DAgger and DSRL improve on the base policy but much less than RLT, which increases throughput by \(2-3\times\).

Q3: How much does each component contribute?

All components contribute (Fig. 7). Replacing the RL token with ResNet-10 cuts throughput by 50%, as does mean pooling instead of the encoder-decoder, showing the compression mechanism matters. Single-step actions instead of chunks (\(C\)=10) fail to match the base policy due to the longer horizon and needed ResNet fallback. Removing the BC regularizer (\(\beta\)=0) degrades performance most, forcing exploration of the full chunked action space. Removing pass-through eventually matches RLT but learns slower, with early exploration drift and occasional jerky motion.

Q4: Does RLT lead to more effective emergent strategies?

On Ethernet, we compare speed distributions of demonstrations, base policy, and RL policy (Fig. 8). The base VLA often "probes" near contact—approaching, retreating, readjusting, retrying. RLT inserts in a fluid motion, and when the first attempt fails, it applies pressure and wiggles the connector to exploit compliance. This behavior is absent from demonstrations and emerges from online exploration.

6 Conclusion and Limitations

RLT enables fast online RL on a compact representation exposed by a large pretrained VLA, letting a lightweight actor-critic improve precise tasks with a few hours of practice. Across four tasks, it improves success and speed, with up to 3× speedup on the hardest phase and, in some cases, faster-than-expert strategies emerging from RL.

RLT requires human involvement for rewards, interventions, and switching between RL and base policies; automating these is future work. The RL token is trained only on task-specific demonstrations, and transfer across tasks and embodiments is open. Using RL-collected experience to improve the foundation model itself is a natural next step. We believe RLT moves toward robots that improve on the job, where pretraining provides an initialization and RL discovers the best strategy.

B.1 Algorithm

Algorithm 1 RLT
Require: Frozen VLA backbone \(f_{\theta_{\text{vla}}}\) and VLA action distribution \(\pi_{\text{vla}}\); demo data \(\mathcal{D}\), chunk length
  \(C\), replay buffer \(\mathcal{B}\), warmup steps \(N_{\text{warm}}\), ratio \(G\), VLA fine-tuning weight \(\alpha\), policy constraint \(\beta\).
 1: Train RL token and (optionally) fine-tune the VLA
 2: Train \(\phi\) using \(\mathbf{z}_i = f_i(\mathbf{s}, \ell, \theta_{\text{vla}})\), \(\mathbf{z}_{\text{rl}} = g_\phi([\mathbf{z}_{1:M}, \mathbf{e}_{\text{rl}}])_{M+1}\), and \(\theta_{\text{vla}}\) (only if \(\alpha > 0\)).
      \[\mathcal{L}_{\text{ro}}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{M}\big\|h_\phi\big(d_\phi([\mathbf{z}_{\text{rl}}, \bar{\mathbf{z}}_{1:i-1}])\big)_i - \bar{\mathbf{z}}_i\big\|^2\Big].\]
 3:   \[\phi, \theta_{\text{vla}} = \arg\min_{\phi,\theta_{\text{vla}}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{ro}}(\phi) + \alpha\mathcal{L}_{\text{vla}}(\theta_{\text{vla}})\]
 4: Train RL actor and critic
 5: Initialize critic \(Q_\psi\) and RL Policy \(\pi_\theta\).
 6: for environment steps \(t = 0, C, 2C \ldots\) do
 7:   Sample VLA reference chunk \(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{t:t+C-1} \sim \pi_{\text{vla}}(\mathbf{s}_t)\).
 8:   Form RL state \(\mathbf{x}_t = (\mathbf{z}_{\text{rl}}(\mathbf{s}_t), \mathbf{s}^p_t)\).
 9:   \(\mathbf{a}_{t:t+C-1} \leftarrow \begin{cases}\mathbf{a}^{\text{human}} & \text{if intervention}\\ \tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{t:t+C-1} & \text{if } t < N_{\text{warm}}\\ \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \tilde{\mathbf{a}}) & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\)
10:   Execute \(\mathbf{a}_{t:t+C-1}\) and observe \(r_t, \mathbf{s}_{t+1}, \mathbf{s}^p_{t+1}\)
11:   \(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{t:t+C-1} \leftarrow \mathbf{a}^{\text{human}}\) if intervention
12:   Store transition in \(\mathcal{B}\): \(\langle \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{a}_{t:t+C-1}, \tilde{\mathbf{a}}, r_t, \mathbf{x}_{t+1}\rangle\)
13:   for \(g = 1, \ldots, G\) do
14:     Sample batch of data b \(\sim \mathcal{B}\).
15:     Compute target Q values
          \[\hat{Q} = \sum_{t'=1}^{C}\gamma^{t'-1} r_{t'} + \gamma^C \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}'\sim\pi_\theta}\big[Q_{\psi'}(\mathbf{x}', \mathbf{a}')\big]\]
16:     Train Critic with TD backup (Eq. (2))
          \[\mathcal{L}_Q(\psi) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{b}}\Big[\big(\hat{Q} - Q_\psi(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{a})\big)^2\Big]\]
17:     Train Policy \(\mathbf{a} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid \mathbf{s}, \tilde{\mathbf{a}})\) (Eq. (3))
          \[\mathcal{L}_\pi(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{b}}\Big[-Q_\psi(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{a}) + \beta\|\mathbf{a} - \tilde{\mathbf{a}}\|_2^2\Big]\]
18:   end for
19: end for

B.2 Additional experiment details

We collect task demonstrations, then fine-tune the VLA and train the RL token for 2000–10000 gradient steps; the VLA is frozen during online RL.

Actor and critic are two-layer MLPs (hidden 256) trained from scratch for zip tie, Ethernet, and charger, and three-layer MLPs (hidden 512) for screw installation. Inputs are the RL token and proprioceptive position and velocity. The critic uses two Q functions with the minimum for targets [19]. The actor also takes the reference chunk, masked 50% in training and always provided at inference, and is a Gaussian with small fixed standard deviation outputting chunk \(\mathbf{a}_{t:t+C-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{C\times d}\) with \(C\)=10. Chunks are subsampled 2 steps apart, giving ~25 samples per second of data. The operator gives a sparse +1 reward on completion.

For screw and zip tie, training starts in the critical-phase setting, then moves to full tasks with the base model handling non-critical phases, improving efficiency and robustness to base-policy-induced states. We report performance after about 5 hours of data.

B.3 Additional experimental details for the baselines

All baselines use the same environment and delta action space at 50 Hz.

PLD: We pre-train the critic on 50 base policy rollouts with Cal-QL [31], then run online RL.

DSRL: Following the authors' code, we predict a \((1, 32)\) dimensional latent action, repeated 50 times to match the VLA's noise input.

HIL-SERL: We initialize RLPD with 20 demonstration episodes and provide interventions, but it failed due to the higher control frequency (50 Hz vs. 10 Hz) and no action-space bounding box.

DAgger: We fine-tune the VLA on demonstrations mixed with the same intervention data from online RL training.
